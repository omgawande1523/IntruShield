1. Problem

IntruShield is a network intrusion detection model that classifies individual connection records as normal or attack (binary task) and assigns attacks to one of four families – DoS, Probe, R2L, U2R (5-class task). Operational priorities: high detection rate, a bounded false-alarm rate (≤ 5 % on normal traffic), and honest measurement of how performance degrades on attack types never seen in training.

3. Preprocessing

A single scikit-learn Pipeline (ColumnTransformer → classifier) is fitted on training rows only, and persisted together with the model, so test rows always pass through exactly the training-time transformation.

  • Categorical (protocol_type, service, flag): OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore") – services seen only in test become all-zero rather than crashing.
  • Heavily skewed counts/bytes (duration, src_bytes, dst_bytes, hot, count, srv_count, dst_host_count, …): log1p.
  • Scaling: StandardScaler on all numeric columns for LogisticRegression only; trees use raw (log-transformed) values.
  • Dropped: constant column(s) num_outbound_cmds; the difficulty score.
  • Imbalance: class_weight="balanced" (LR, LightGBM), balanced_subsample (RandomForest), balanced sample_weight (XGBoost).

Leakage discipline: KDDTrain+ was split 85/15 stratified on the 5-class label (107,077 / 18,896 rows, random_state=42). Hyper-parameter search (RandomizedSearchCV, 12 configurations × 3-fold, macro-F1) ran on the 85 % part, model selection and the decision threshold used only the 15 % validation part. KDDTest+ / KDDTest-21 were loaded for the first time in evaluate.py.

6. Why the gap exists – unseen attacks

For LightGBM the binary macro-F1 drops from 0.9732 (Protocol A) to 0.8969 (KDDTest+). Splitting KDDTest+ attacks by whether their attack type ever occurred in KDDTrain+ explains the drop:

ModelSeen types: nSeen: detectedSeen: recallUnseen types: nUnseen: detectedUnseen: recall
LogisticRegression9,0837,05977.7%3,7501,32935.4%
RandomForest9,0839,03099.4%3,7503,56395.0%
XGBoost9,0838,27491.1%3,7503,21385.7%
LightGBM9,0838,36292.1%3,7503,22886.1%

LightGBM detects 92.1% of attacks whose type it has seen but only 86.1% of the 3,750 connections belonging to the 17 unseen types. In addition, several seen R2L types (e.g. guess_passwd, warezmaster) appear in test with far higher frequency and different feature profiles than in training, and many R2L/U2R connections are content-level attacks that look like normal logins at the connection-feature level. Nothing in this gap is a bug: it is the covariate and label shift NSL-KDD was built to expose, and it is the honest number to quote for deployment.

9. Threshold & False Alarms

The operating point was chosen on the validation split only: maximise attack recall subject to a false-alarm rate ≤ 5%. Recall can only fall as the threshold rises, so the optimum is the lowest threshold whose validation FAR still respects the budget. Because the validation split is almost perfectly separable, this rule spends the whole false-alarm budget on sensitivity – deliberately, since the detector must face attack types that never occur in validation. Result: threshold 0.00062 on P(attack) → validation recall 100.00%, validation false-alarm rate 5.00%. On KDDTest+ the same threshold gives recall 90.63% and false-alarm rate 9.72% (944 of 9,711 normal connections flagged).

Validation cannot distinguish thresholds between 10-6 and 0.5 (recall and FAR barely move), whereas on KDDTest+ the choice moves recall by tens of points: unseen attacks receive low but non-zero attack probabilities, so a sensitive threshold catches many of them at the cost of a higher false-alarm rate under distribution shift.

11. Limitations & Next Steps

  • Dataset age and realism. NSL-KDD derives from 1998/99 DARPA traffic; services, attack tooling and traffic mixes have changed. Numbers here measure method quality, not expected performance on a modern network.
  • Unseen attacks. Supervised classifiers can only generalise from feature similarity; zero-day detection needs anomaly / novelty detection (e.g. isolation forest or autoencoder on normal traffic) alongside the classifier.
  • R2L / U2R. With few hundred training rows and strong distribution shift, per-class recall is low. Options: targeted oversampling or focal loss, hierarchical models (attack → family), or cost-sensitive thresholds per class.
  • Thresholding. The threshold maximises recall under a 5 % validation FAR; because validation is in-distribution, the test FAR differs. Calibrating on a small labelled sample of target traffic would tighten this.
  • Tuning budget. 12 random configurations × 3 folds per booster to stay within the 25-minute CPU budget; a larger search or early-stopping tuning may add a little macro-F1 on Protocol A but is unlikely to close the Protocol B gap.
  • Next steps. Evaluate on CIC-IDS2017 / UNSW-NB15, add an anomaly-score channel, estimate calibration (Brier / reliability), and package the pipeline as a streaming scorer with drift monitoring on the top SHAP features.
